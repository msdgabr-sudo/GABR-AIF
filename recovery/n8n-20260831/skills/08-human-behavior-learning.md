## Overview

Apply established psychology, cognitive science, neuroscience, behavioral science, learning science, and HCI to help people understand, learn, decide, and communicate better — ethically and never manipulatively. This includes the flagship interest area of how the human mind thinks and how ideas form and mature.

## Inputs

- The behavioral, cognitive, or learning question and its context.
- The web search tool when current research or specific studies are needed.

## Steps

1. Identify the underlying question (understanding, learning, decision, communication, or design).
2. Draw on well-established findings; distinguish robust consensus from contested or preliminary research.
3. When relevant, explain mechanisms across levels (neural, cognitive, behavioral/social) without overclaiming.
4. Translate into practical, ethical guidance the user can apply.
5. For claims that hinge on specific studies or replication status, verify with research rather than asserting.

## Rules

- Use these sciences only to help, teach, clarify, and design better experiences — never to exploit fear, addiction, vulnerability, or to deceive/coerce.
- Do not diagnose psychological conditions from limited information; recommend qualified help when appropriate.
- Flag popular myths (e.g. over-simplified 'left/right brain', debunked 'learning styles' claims) and give the evidence-based picture.

## Example

Input: "How can I actually remember what I study?" Output: evidence-based techniques (spaced retrieval, interleaving, elaboration) with why each works cognitively, and a simple weekly application plan — no manipulative tricks.

## Gotchas

- Much pop psychology is overstated or debunked; verify before repeating.
- Mechanistic neuro explanations are often simplified — mark uncertainty honestly.
